THE UNCOMFORTABLE TRUTH

Hackathons aren't won by code.

  • No judge spends more than ~30 seconds looking at your code. They score what they see on screen.
  • The demo is the product. A polished demo of one feature beats a broken demo of five.
  • Your first real customer is the judge. Build for them, not for the leaderboard of sponsor tracks.
  • Using AI is normal now. The edge isn't the tool — it's the problem you chose.

RULE 01 · SCOPE

Scope down. Then down again.

  • Your initial idea is always too big. Cut it in half. Then cut it in half again.
  • One feature that works end-to-end beats five that half-work. Every time.
  • If a feature isn't in the demo path, it's a candidate to cut. Hardcode or stub it.
  • Over-scoping is the #1 reason teams don't place. The idea feels achievable at hour 2, impossible at hour 30.

RULE 02 · DEMO-FIRST

Build the demo first. Features second.

  • Work backwards from the 5-minute demo. What must appear on screen? Build only that.
  • Define the happy path: the exact sequence you'll walk through, tested and known not to break.
  • Get a working skeleton by the first quarter — hour 6 of 24, hour 12 of 48. Ugly is fine.
  • A skeleton that runs end-to-end exposes integration bugs while there's still time to fix them.
05 / 15
RULE 03 · SCRIPT

Write the demo script before the meaningful code.

1

Problem

One sentence. The pain, in plain language, around a specific person.

2

Before state

What someone does today — the workaround and its cost.

3

Trigger

The exact action that starts the demo. Judges know what to watch for.

4

Aha moment

The single thing every judge remembers after you leave.

5

Close

Tie it to scale or impact in one line.

If you can't write this script, you don't yet know what you're building. Stop and figure it out.

06 / 15
RULE 04 · PITCH

Pitch the problem harder than the solution.

  • The winning split is roughly 30% problem / 70% solution. Most teams do 5% / 95%.
  • Use a specific person, not a category: "a backend dev at a Series B SaaS who spends 2 hours a week on X."
  • Spend 20–30 seconds validating the problem. Don't assume judges will just get it.
  • Tell a story, then show the app. Don't get lost in the technical weeds.
07 / 15
RULE 05 · IDEA

Pick a problem you actually have.

  • Personal pain points win. You get the problem, the solution sketch, and the customer — yourself.
  • Colleen's trick: don't keep a list of hackathon ideas. Keep a list of things that annoy you.
  • Start from the theme or track, then work backwards. Judges can tell when a project wasn't built for them.
  • Don't force originality. Ask "what do I wish existed that doesn't?" — then build that.
08 / 15
RULE 06 · TEAM

Two to three people. Clear roles.

A

Builder

Owns the happy path end to end.

B

Designer

UI, visual wow, realistic data.

C

Presenter

Owns the deck from day one. Practices the pitch.

  • One person owns the presentation from day one — not at hour 23.
  • Everyone must be able to explain the whole project. Judges spot the passenger.
  • Leave one chair open. Talk to mentors and organizers early.
09 / 15
RULE 07 · STACK

Stick to what you know. Use the sponsors.

  • Don't learn a new framework mid-event. Speed comes from familiarity.
  • Talk to sponsors before building. They'll tell you what's boring vs. innovative.
  • Load their MCP servers and skills into your agent up front — build correctly the first time.
  • Use coding agents liberally (~5 in parallel), but read everything they produce.
  • Mock data feeds and third-party APIs. Never mock the feature judges came to see.
10 / 15
RULE 08 · LOCK

Lock the code four hours before submission.

  • Freeze features. Fix bugs. Rehearse. New features in the final stretch break what already shipped.
  • Hardcode the happy path. Mock external services. Cache responses.
  • Keep a backup: record the demo twice, grab screenshots as fallback.
  • Write the README so judges who don't see your demo can still evaluate it.
  • Submit at least 30 minutes early. The buzzer is when problems show up.
11 / 15
RULE 09 · POLISH

Make it look good. Make it real.

  • Visual wow factor is universal — judges can't assess technical depth in 3 minutes.
  • Use realistic data, not "Lorem ipsum." Placeholder data looks unfinished.
  • Check it works on a phone. Some judges will open it there.
  • Add one memorable touch — a sound, a surprise ending, a clever use of the theme. After the core works.
  • Make it usable without explanation: start screen, short instructions, buttons that look like buttons.
12 / 15
RULE 10 · HABITS

Sleep. Submit early. Ship a README.

  • The team that sleeps 4–5 hours and presents with energy beats the all-nighter. Always.
  • Practice the demo with another team. Find where it breaks before judges do.
  • Keep your main branch functional. Risky features go on feature branches.
  • Answer Q&A honestly: "we didn't have time, but here's how we'd do it" beats bluffing.
  • Be honest about AI-assisted work. Disclose recycled code. Explain how you used it.
13 / 15
ANTI-PATTERNS

What losers do.

  • Coding right up to the buzzer with nothing left for the demo.
  • Five features, none working end to end.
  • An all-nighter and an exhausted pitch.
  • Mocking the core feature judges came to see.
  • Generic "AI for X" with no real problem and no real user.
  • Assuming judges will "just get it." They won't.
